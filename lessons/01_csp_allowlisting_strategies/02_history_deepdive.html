The starting point: host-based allowlisting

CSP's earliest, simplest form of script-src allowlisting was exactly what Module 1's baseline made obvious the absence of: a list of trusted origins (script-src 'self' https://cdn.example.com). The mental model was straightforward — decide which domains you trust, list them, done.

Why that model broke down in practice

Security researchers (notably a widely-cited Google study, "CSP Is Dead, Long Live CSP!", presented at ACM CCS 2016) analyzed real-world CSP deployments and found the overwhelming majority of host-based policies could be bypassed. The core problem wasn't sloppy policy-writing — it was structural: almost any sufficiently popular CDN or script host also serves *something* an attacker can abuse once it's allowlisted — a JSONP endpoint that reflects arbitrary callback names into executable script, an old library version with a known gadget, an "upload a file" feature that happens to live on the same origin. Allowlisting a whole origin means trusting everything that origin will EVER serve, including things nobody anticipated when the policy was written.

The pivot: trust content or a secret, not a whole origin

Nonces and hashes (Modules 5–6) sidestep that entire problem class: they don't ask "do I trust this origin," they ask "do I trust this EXACT piece of content, right now." A hash can't be satisfied by ANY script on an allowlisted CDN — only the one exact byte sequence it was computed from. A nonce can't be guessed or reused from a different response. Both close the "I trusted the origin, not knowing everything it could ever serve" gap that made host allowlisting fragile.

Why that still wasn't enough for real, JS-heavy applications

Nonces and hashes work cleanly for a small, known set of inline scripts. They work far less cleanly for the reality of a modern JS-heavy application: bundlers, ad networks, and analytics/widget scripts that dynamically insert an unpredictable, often large number of further <script> tags at runtime. Hand-nonceing or hand-hashing every one of those isn't practical — the same research that diagnosed host-allowlisting's weakness also identified this as the reason teams reached for 'unsafe-inline' anyway, defeating CSP's protection to keep their app working.

'strict-dynamic' (Module 7) is the direct answer to that specific gap: keep the nonce/hash-based trust root (still no whole-origin trust), but let code you've already decided to trust propagate that trust forward to what IT loads, without needing individual credentials for every downstream script. It didn't replace nonces/hashes — it's built on top of them, solving the one thing they couldn't scale to.

The pattern, zoomed out

Each mechanism exists because the previous one had a real, specific, documented failure mode in production use — not because standards bodies enjoy adding options. That's worth remembering the next time a security feature seems to have more knobs than you'd expect: the knobs are usually scar tissue from a real, previously-exploited gap.
