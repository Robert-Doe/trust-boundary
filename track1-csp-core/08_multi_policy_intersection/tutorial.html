01 — The Big Analogy

Picture two separate security checkpoints on the road into a facility, run by two different agencies that never talk to each other. Checkpoint 1 has a guest list and waves through anyone on it. Checkpoint 2 has its own, shorter guest list. To actually reach the building, your name has to be on both lists — being on Checkpoint 1's list only gets you past Checkpoint 1. Neither checkpoint's list "helps" the other one; they don't merge into one combined list. You're stopped the moment you fail either checkpoint, regardless of how generously the other one would have treated you.

02 — What Happens, Step by Step

  1. Server sends two separate Content-Security-Policy header lines on one response — not one policy with two clauses, two genuinely independent policies.
  2. Browser parses both, keeps them as two separate active policies (not merged into one internal representation).
  3. For each load attempt, the browser checks it against Policy 1 in full, and separately against Policy 2 in full.
  4. The load is only allowed if it passes both checks. A single failing policy blocks it, no matter how permissive the other policy is.
  5. Each violation event reports exactly which policy string caused that specific block, via event.originalPolicy.

04 — The Code, Explained

Sending two real header lines — server.js:

res.setHeader('Content-Security-Policy', [POLICY_1, POLICY_2]);

Capturing which policy did the blocking, not just that something blocked — public/violation-listener.js:

window.__cspViolations.push({
  blockedURI: e.blockedURI,
  violatedDirective: e.violatedDirective,
  originalPolicy: e.originalPolicy, // the exact policy string that blocked THIS load
});

05 — Limits / What You Cannot Do Yet

✗ CAN'T DO YET

  • Use a second, looser policy to "relax" a strict one — combination is AND, never OR
  • Combine a header policy with a `<meta>`-tag policy the exact same way — Module 9 covers real differences there
  • Assume an unset directive means "unrestricted" in general — it depends on that specific policy's own `default-src`

✓ CAN DO

  • Layer a stricter policy on top of an existing one without touching the original (useful for defense-in-depth added by, say, a CDN or reverse proxy)
  • Identify exactly which policy blocked a given load using `event.originalPolicy`
  • Reason correctly about "missing directive" as meaning "this policy doesn't constrain it," not "this policy forbids it"

Getting a blank or broken result? If you only see ONE Content-Security-Policy header in your network tab instead of two, check that you used res.setHeader(name, [a, b]) — passing a single comma-joined string instead produces one malformed policy, not two valid ones, and every proof's behavior will look different from what's documented here.

07 — Brain Exercise

Think it through before revealing

A CDN sits in front of your app server and injects its own baseline Content-Security-Policy header for every response, on top of whatever your app already sends. Your app team wants to loosen their own policy temporarily to debug something in production. Will adding 'unsafe-inline' to your app's policy actually let inline scripts run, given the CDN's header is still attached too?

Reveal the answer:

Only if the CDN's policy also allows it — which, per this module, is unlikely to be what a security-conscious CDN's baseline policy does. Since policies combine by AND, loosening YOUR policy can only ever expand what your policy alone would allow; it can never override or loosen a restriction imposed by the other active policy. This is a genuinely common production gotcha: teams add `'unsafe-inline'` to debug something, see no change, and conclude their header update didn't deploy — when actually it deployed fine, but a second, stricter policy from infrastructure they don't control is still in the mix.

08 — There Are No Dumb Questions

Q: Is this the same rule as Module 2's "duplicate directive: first wins"?

No — genuinely different mechanisms, easy to conflate. Module 2's rule is about TWO DIRECTIVES WITH THE SAME NAME inside ONE policy string (`script-src a; script-src b` — second ignored). This module is about TWO ENTIRELY SEPARATE POLICIES, each fully parsed and enforced on its own, then combined by requiring both to pass. A duplicate directive within one policy never reaches the "combine multiple policies" step at all — it's resolved before that.

Q: Why would a real site ever send two policies instead of just writing one stricter policy?

The most common real reason: they don't control both. A CDN, reverse proxy, or platform layer might add its own baseline security header independent of the application's own CSP — the app team can't necessarily edit or even see the infrastructure layer's policy, and vice versa. Intersection is what makes "layered, independently-authored policies" a coherent security model instead of a race condition.

Q: Does the order of the two header lines matter — would swapping Policy 1 and Policy 2 change any result?

No — intersection (logical AND) is commutative. We didn't test this by relying on documentation alone being trustworthy, but it follows directly from "a load must pass every active policy": there's no step in that description where order could matter, unlike Module 2's duplicate-directive rule, where order (first vs. second) is the entire rule.
